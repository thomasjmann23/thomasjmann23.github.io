## Executive summary

A small business's bank account shows, day by day, how much money comes in, how much goes out and how close the balance runs to zero. Lenders have always looked at bank statements, but usually as a check at the end of a credit decision. Cash-flow underwriting turns that data into structured variables and puts them into the decision itself, sometimes alongside a credit score and sometimes in place of one. This paper sets out what is publicly known about cash-flow underwriting for small-business loans in the United States as of November 2026: the evidence that it predicts default, the vendors and banks selling or using it, the rules on getting the data, and what all of this means for lenders and credit teams. It separates what companies say about their own products from what independent research and regulators confirm.

Key findings:

- **The independent evidence says cash-flow data helps, modestly.** The best public study, run by FinRegLab with academic researchers on about 38,000 small-business loans from two fintech lenders, found that adding about two dozen bank-account variables to a credit-score model raised ROC AUC from 0.652 to 0.663 [2][3]. A related NBER working paper on the same lenders found gains of 0.015 to 0.017 [5]. These are real but small improvements, and the two studies share researchers and, it appears, data.
- **The gains are concentrated where credit scores are weakest.** Among borrowers whose predicted risk changed most, the cash-flow model moved 3.6 low-score borrowers to substantially lower risk for every one it moved to higher risk, and 7.6 for low-score owners of businesses under five years old [3]. The NBER paper finds younger owners with lower scores gain approval at lenders that lean on cash flow, while high-score young owners lose slightly [5].
- **Vendor performance claims are much larger and are not comparable.** Experian says its new commercial Cashflow Attributes give "up to" a 24% lift in predictive performance [18]. Plaid says LendScore 2 has 42% more predictive power than traditional credit data [23]. Neither defines the baseline in a way that can be checked, neither has been independently validated, and Plaid's and FICO's scores are consumer products, not business ones [21][22][23].
- **The SBA has opened the door.** Since March 1, 2026, SBA no longer uses the FICO SBSS score to screen 7(a) Small loans. Lenders must instead analyse credit history, show a debt service coverage ratio of at least 1.10 to 1 and review the two most recent months of bank statements on the operating account. A lender may use its own scoring model if its regulator allows it and the model does not rely solely on consumer credit scores [7][8]. That wording leaves room for cash-flow models, although the notices do not mention them.
- **Business accounts have no federal data-access right.** The CFPB's 2024 open-banking rule under Section 1033 covers consumer accounts only. The CFPB explicitly declined to extend it to small businesses [11]. The rule is in any case enjoined and being rewritten: a new proposal went to White House review in August 2026 and had not been published in the sources found [12][14][15]. Lenders getting small-business account data rely on contracts with aggregators and banks, which have started charging for access [16][17].
- **The fair-lending guardrails have moved.** The CFPB withdrew its 2022 circular on adverse-action reasons for complex models in May 2025 [27], removed disparate-impact provisions from Regulation B effective July 2026 [28][29], and narrowed the Section 1071 small-business data rule to large lenders, with first collection in 2028 [30]. The statutory duty to give specific reasons for a denial remains, as do state laws and the Fair Housing Act. The public data that could show who gains and who loses from cash-flow models is shrinking.

## Background

### What cash-flow underwriting means

Traditional small-business underwriting combines three sources: the owner's personal credit report and score, the business's financial statements and tax returns, and, for larger loans, collateral. For small loans, many lenders lean most on the personal score because financial statements are slow to get and hard to verify. The FDIC's survey of banks found that among large banks, 59% ranked credit bureau information as the most important factor for small loans, against 11% of small banks, which put the applicant's financial position first [6].

Cash-flow underwriting adds a fourth source: transaction-level data from the business's deposit accounts. From several months of transactions a lender can compute monthly deposits and withdrawals, average and minimum balances, how volatile those figures are, how often the account runs low or negative, how many payments bounce, and how much goes out each day to merchant cash advances or other daily-pay debt [3]. These variables then enter a scorecard, a machine-learning model, a rule set or an analyst's review.

None of this is new to commercial credit. Analysts have long read statements to check that deposits match reported revenue. What has changed is scale and format. Aggregators can now deliver 12 or 24 months of categorised transactions in seconds, and document tools can convert uploaded PDF statements into the same structure. That makes it practical to use account data on a $50,000 loan, not only on a $5 million one.

### Why small businesses are the obvious case

The Federal Reserve Banks' 2025 Small Business Credit Survey, published in March 2026, collected 6,525 responses from employer firms. It found that 38% had applied for a loan, line of credit or merchant cash advance in the prior year. Across all financing types, 42% of applicants got everything they asked for and 22% got nothing [1]. Among those not fully approved, the most common reasons given were that requirements were too strict (46%), the business already had too much debt (37%), a low credit score (30%), insufficient collateral (29%) and weak sales (29%) [1].

![Bar chart of the reasons small-business applicants say they were not approved for financing](research/cash-flow-underwriting-small-business/fig1-denials.svg)

*Figure 1. Reasons given by employer firms that were not approved for at least some of the financing they sought, 2025 Small Business Credit Survey [1]. Respondents could give more than one reason. The SBCS is a weighted convenience sample, not a random survey. Chart by the author.*

Three of those reasons (debt load, credit score and sales) are things bank-account data can measure directly or test against. A personal credit score says little about a two-year-old business with strong, steady deposits. It says even less about a young owner with a thin personal file. This is the argument cash-flow advocates make, and it is the claim the research below tests.

The survey also shows where demand has gone. The share of applicants who sought financing from online lenders rose from 17% in 2020 to 29% in 2025. Online lenders have long underwritten from bank data, but 60% of their borrowers said costs turned out higher than expected, and their net satisfaction was far below that of banks and credit unions [1]. Cash-flow underwriting is therefore not new to small businesses. What is new is the prospect of it moving from high-cost online lenders into banks, bureaus and the SBA programmes.

## What the evidence shows

### The FinRegLab study

The strongest public evidence on small-business cash-flow underwriting is FinRegLab's June 2025 study, "Sharpening the Focus: Using Cash-Flow Data to Underwrite Financially Constrained Businesses" [2][3]. FinRegLab is a nonprofit research centre. The study was done with Sabrina Howell and Siena Matsumoto, then of NYU Stern, and funded by a Minority Business Development Agency grant with support from Visa and Plaid, which provided account connectivity for related pilots [2].

The data covered about 38,000 small-business loans from two unnamed fintech lenders, originated between February 2015 and January 2024, excluding PPP and other special programmes [3]. The researchers built baseline models using the owner's personal credit score, industry, firm age and other basic variables, then added about two dozen cash-flow metrics: transformed deposits, withdrawals and balances, the volatility of deposits and balances, ratios, low or negative balance events, insufficient-funds incidents and daily-pay loans such as merchant cash advances [3].

Three results matter:

1. **The overall gain is small.** For the machine-learning model, ROC AUC rose from 0.652 to 0.663 [3]. FinRegLab describes this as almost 2%; in absolute terms it is 0.011.
2. **The gain is larger for weaker credit files.** Improvements were somewhat bigger for owners with scores below 700, and particularly large for low-score owners of businesses less than five years old [3].
3. **Reclassification favours low-score borrowers.** Among borrowers whose risk estimate moved most, the cash-flow model moved 3.6 times as many low-score borrowers to substantially lower risk as to substantially higher risk. For low-score early-stage firms, the ratio was 7.6 [3].

The most predictive variables were the level and volatility of deposits and balances, followed by withdrawals and low or negative balance events. Insufficient-funds incidents and daily-pay loans contributed less [3]. A supplementary analysis of low-score owners in lower-income zip codes with more Black and Hispanic residents found some stronger effects, but FinRegLab says these were mainly driven by the general benefit to low-score owners rather than by location [3].

FinRegLab's earlier work, published around 2019, used data from six non-bank providers (Accion, Brigit, Kabbage, LendUp, Oportun and Petal) and an independent analysis by Charles River Associates to test whether cash-flow variables predicted performance [4]. That project covered mostly consumer lending and is less directly relevant here.

### The NBER paper

A companion NBER working paper by Christopher Hair, Sabrina Howell, Mark Johnson and Siena Matsumoto, "Modernizing Access to Credit for Younger Entrepreneurs: From FICO to Cash Flow," goes further [5]. It was first issued in January 2025 and revised in September 2025, and it has not been peer reviewed.

The paper uses three anonymous fintech companies. Lender A is an online lender with automated scoring that took bank data through Plaid. Lender B is an online lender whose loan officers underwrite manually and which screens out FICO scores below 660; it took bank data through Ocrolus. The third is a platform that routes applications to 111 partner lenders, including banks [5]. Default analysis uses 38,021 loans from Lenders A and B, very close to FinRegLab's 38,000, which suggests the two studies draw on the same loan data. They should be read as one body of evidence, not two.

The paper's findings:

- **Predictive power.** A random-forest model on cash-flow variables beat a FICO-based model by 0.017 AUC in a minimal specification (0.617 to 0.634) and 0.015 in the preferred one (0.642 to 0.657). Adding loan pricing as a feature narrowed the gap to 0.009 [5].
- **Approval at cash-flow-intensive lenders.** The authors classified 53 of 76 platform lenders with enough data as "cash-flow intensive", meaning cash-flow variables improved prediction of their decisions. Comparing the same application sent to both kinds of lender, applicants under 40 were 2.4 percentage points more likely to be approved at a cash-flow-intensive lender, about 12% of the mean approval rate of roughly 20%. Across all applications, without that restriction, the effect was 1.4 points [5]. The estimate is sensitive to sample choice.
- **A natural experiment.** At Lender B, applications were randomly assigned to loan officers. For applicants under 40 with below-median FICO scores, being assigned to an officer who leaned on cash flow raised approval by about 9 percentage points, against a mean of 48% [5]. This rests on 15 loan officers.
- **Winners and losers.** Using a method they call TACO, the authors show that young owners with FICO scores below 740 are over-represented among those the cash-flow model helps, while young owners with scores of 740 or above are over-represented among those it hurts [5]. Cash-flow underwriting reallocates credit; it does not only add it.
- **Risk.** Business survival, measured by whether the firm still appeared open on Google in September 2024, was the same (42%) for applicants at cash-flow-intensive and other platform lenders. The authors read this as evidence that the approval gains did not come from taking more risk [5]. Survival is a weak proxy for repayment.

### What the evidence does not show

The public evidence has clear limits:

- **It comes from fintech lenders.** No public study tests cash-flow variables on a bank's small-business book, on SBA loans, or on loans above a few hundred thousand dollars. The FDIC survey shows banks underwrite differently, with small banks relying more on financial position and relationships [6].
- **The gains are measured against a thin baseline.** Both studies compare cash-flow variables with a personal credit score plus basic firm data. A bank that already analyses tax returns and debt service coverage starts from a stronger baseline, and the marginal value of account data may be smaller.
- **No study measures losses in dollars.** AUC and reclassification ratios show ranking ability. They do not tell a credit committee how charge-offs or yields would change at a given approval rate.
- **The data period ends before generative-AI document forgery became common.** If account data arrives as uploaded PDFs, the inputs themselves can be edited. This site's [study of AI-forged documents](research.html?id=ai-forged-documents-small-business-lending) found that bank statements are the most targeted document in small-business lending. Data pulled directly from the bank is far harder to fake.

## The market: who sells what

### Bureaus and scores

**Experian** has made the largest recent push. On September 29, 2026 it launched Cashflow Attributes for commercial lenders: more than 500 attributes built from business bank transaction data, covering liquidity, revenue trends, operating expenses, debt exposure and repayment capacity [18]. The data can be a lender's own checking-account data on its customers or permissioned commercial account data. Experian names Slope, a business decisioning and commercial account data company, as its technology partner [18]. Experian's own preliminary analysis claims up to a 24% lift in predictive performance when the attributes are added to commercial credit data, especially for thin-file businesses [18][19]. A week later, Experian announced the Cashflow Data Bureau, a new consumer reporting agency for consumer-permissioned bank data, claiming up to 40% better prediction and up to 25% more approvals at the same risk, measured by Gini against conventional scores [20]. No customers were named in either release.

**FICO** announced a next-generation UltraFICO score with Plaid in November 2025. It maps consumer checking, savings and money-market cash flow onto the FICO scale and is distributed through Plaid Check, Plaid's consumer reporting agency [21]. The release gave no performance figures and does not mention business use [21].

The SBA's own small-loan score is going the other way. FICO's Small Business Scoring Service (SBSS), which blends business and personal credit data, was the SBA's screen for 7(a) Small loans; a lender-side source reports that SOP 50 10 8 raised the minimum from 155 to 165 in mid-2025 [9]. From March 1, 2026, SBA stopped using it altogether [7][8]. Experian's commentary on the change mentions guarantor and blended credit data but not account data [33].

### Data networks

**Plaid** launched LendScore in beta in October 2025: a 1 to 99 consumer score for unsecured lending built from cash-flow data and what Plaid calls network insights, such as which financial apps a person connects to and for how long. It comes with adverse-action reason codes and a fairness review by FairPlay [22]. In October 2026 Plaid announced LendScore 2, rescaled to 1,000 to 2,000 and claimed to have 42% more predictive power than traditional credit data, plus specialised versions for auto, home lending and short-term credit, and a transformer-based model called LendScore Arc [23]. All of these are consumer products and all figures are Plaid's own. Plaid's fall 2026 release says cash flow is now a first look, not a second one, but it does not mention small-business lending [23].

**Nova Credit** supplies Cash Atlas, a tool that scores applicants from bank-account behaviour. JPMorganChase said in September 2025 that it would use Cash Atlas, along with foreign credit bureau data, in some credit decisions [24]. The public reporting does not say whether this covers small-business credit or whether the bank uses its own deposit data, permissioned data or both.

### Document analysis

Where account data is not available by connection, lenders work from uploaded statements. Companies such as Ocrolus convert PDF statements into structured transactions; the NBER paper says one of its lenders and the routing platform received bank data this way [5]. This route works for any bank, but it depends on the PDF being genuine.

The vendor figures cannot be compared with the research figures or with each other. "Lift" can mean relative change in Gini, KS or AUC, measured on a chosen segment, at a chosen cut-off. A 24% relative lift in Gini on a thin-file segment and a 0.011 AUC gain on a full book could describe similar underlying improvements. Without the baseline, segment and metric, a credit team cannot tell.

## Getting the data: access rules

*Figure 3. Three data routes. SBA two-month statement requirement from Procedural Notice 5000-876777 [8]; Section 1033 coverage from the 2024 final rule [11]; injunction and reconsideration from [12][14]. Diagram by the author.*

### Section 1033 does not cover business accounts

The CFPB finalised its Personal Financial Data Rights rule in October 2024 [10][11]. It requires data providers (banks, card issuers and some payment companies) to give consumers and authorised third parties free access to at least 24 months of transaction data, balances and other account information through a developer interface [10]. It phases in by size, from April 2026 for the largest banks to April 2030 for banks above $850 million in assets; smaller depositories are exempt [10].

For small-business lending the key point is scope. The rule covers Regulation E accounts and Regulation Z credit cards, defines a consumer as a natural person (including guardians and trustees acting for one) and is limited by statute to consumer financial products [11]. A commenter asked the CFPB to include small businesses. The Bureau declined, saying that would be inconsistent with the statute, and suggested only that business account providers might find the framework useful on a voluntary basis [11]. Morrison Foerster notes that the first phase also leaves small-business lenders out as data providers [32], and an industry analyst describes business accounts as explicitly excluded [31]. Sole proprietors who run the business through a personal account may be covered in practice; a business checking account is not.

### The rule is enjoined and being rewritten

Even for consumer accounts the rule is not in force. Banks and trade groups sued in the Eastern District of Kentucky. In 2025 the CFPB told the court it considered the rule unlawful, opened a reconsideration with an advance notice in August 2025, and the court enjoined enforcement until that is finished [12][13]. A proposed replacement, "Personal Financial Data Rights Reconsideration", was received for White House regulatory review on August 4, 2026 and is classed as economically significant [14][15]. Its content had not been published in the sources reviewed for this paper, and no Federal Register proposal was found. The main open questions include who may act as an authorised third party and whether data providers may charge fees [14].

### Banks have started charging

In July 2025 JPMorganChase told data aggregators it would begin charging for access to customer data it had previously provided free. In September 2025 Plaid agreed to an extended data-access agreement with JPMorgan that includes a pricing structure; neither company disclosed the terms [16]. Trade press later reported, citing unnamed sources, that JPMorgan reached paid agreements with several other aggregators after lowering its initial demands [17]. These deals concern consumer data, but the same logic applies with more force to business accounts, where there is no rule at all. For a lender, permissioned business account data is a commercial input whose price and availability are set by contract.

### The SBA statement rule

For SBA 7(a) Small loans (up to $350,000), the SBA now requires the two most recent months of commercial bank activity or statements on the primary operating account, used to verify debts and support the debt service calculation, with exemptions for start-ups with no commercial debt and some change-of-ownership deals [8]. The notice does not say how the statements must be obtained. A lender can meet it with uploaded PDFs, its own deposit data or an aggregator feed. Two months is a verification check, not a cash-flow model: it is too short to measure seasonality or volatility.

## Regulation and fair lending

### Guidance that existed

In December 2019, the Fed, CFPB, FDIC, NCUA and OCC issued a joint statement encouraging responsible use of alternative data in underwriting. It named cash-flow data specifically and said its use could raise or lower consumer protection risk depending on how it is done [25]. In May 2022, the CFPB's Circular 2022-03 said that creditors using complex algorithms must still give the specific principal reasons for adverse action and cannot rely on the model's opacity as an excuse [26].

### What changed in 2025 and 2026

- **Circular 2022-03 was withdrawn.** It was among 67 guidance documents the CFPB withdrew on May 12, 2025, according to a summary of the notice [27]. This research could not confirm whether the 2019 interagency statement, a joint document, was affected. The underlying duty to give specific reasons comes from ECOA and Regulation B itself and was not withdrawn.
- **Regulation B dropped disparate impact.** A final rule published April 22, 2026 and effective July 21, 2026 removes the regulation's support for disparate-impact (effects test) liability, narrows discouragement to statements showing intent to discriminate, and restricts for-profit special purpose credit programmes [28][29]. Facially neutral criteria now violate ECOA under the regulation only where they serve as proxies applied with discriminatory intent [28]. Law firms expect legal challenges, and disparate impact remains available under the Fair Housing Act and some state laws [29].
- **Section 1071 was narrowed.** The final small-business lending data rule, issued May 1, 2026, applies only to lenders that made at least 1,000 covered small-business loans in each of the two prior years, defines a small business as one with revenue of $1 million or less, starts collection on January 1, 2028, and drops denial reasons and pricing from the data collected [30].

### Why this matters for cash-flow models

Cash-flow variables are often presented as fairer than credit scores, and the FinRegLab and NBER results support that for young and low-score owners [3][5]. But account data also carries proxies. Deposits and balances track wealth, which differs across groups. Transaction categories can reveal religion (donations), health (pharmacies) or national origin (remittances). Network signals of the kind Plaid uses, such as how many earned-wage-access apps a person connects, reflect financial stress that is not evenly distributed [22].

Under the 2026 rules a lender is less exposed to an effects-based claim from federal regulators. It is still exposed to state law, to private litigation and to its own model risk standards. And it still owes every declined applicant specific reasons. For a model with hundreds of attributes, reasons such as "deposit volatility too high" or "frequent low balances" are explainable to a borrower in a way that a network-derived embedding may not be.

The loss of data matters too. Section 1071 will no longer collect denial reasons or pricing, will not start until 2028, and will cover only the largest lenders [30]. The SBCS is a convenience sample [1]. If cash-flow models spread through banks, there will be little public data to show whether they widen or narrow gaps.

## Implications for lenders and credit teams

**Start with your own deposit data.** A bank that holds the operating account of a small-business applicant already has the cleanest, hardest-to-forge cash-flow data available, with no aggregator fee and no consent flow. Experian's commercial product is explicitly designed to work on a lender's first-party checking data [18]. For existing customers, building a small set of transparent attributes (average and minimum balances, deposit trend and volatility, overdraft and returned-item counts, daily-pay debt outflows) is a modest data project with a clear use.

**Treat vendor lift claims as hypotheses.** Ask for the metric (AUC, Gini, KS), the baseline model, the segment, the performance window and the outcome definition. Test on your own book with an out-of-time sample. The independent evidence suggests a realistic gain on a full small-business book is in the range of 0.01 to 0.02 AUC over a score-based baseline, more for thin files and young firms, and probably less over a full financial-statement review [3][5].

**Use cash flow where it does most work.** The research points to the same segments every time: young businesses, owners with thin or low personal scores, and small loans where full financial analysis is too costly [3][5]. Those are the segments where an SBA 7(a) Small lender, now free of the SBSS screen, needs an alternative to a personal score [7][8].

**Write the SBA use carefully.** The new 7(a) Small rules allow a lender's own scoring model if its regulator permits it, it is used for similar non-SBA loans, and it does not rely solely on consumer scores; the score and approval range must be stated in the credit memo [8]. A cash-flow model can fit those conditions, but nothing in the notices says so. A lender should document the model, its validation and its use on conventional loans before relying on it for SBA loans, and expect the SBA to ask for it.

**Prefer direct data to PDFs.** A model trained on bank-fed transactions will be fed forged inputs if production data comes from uploaded statements. Where PDFs are unavoidable, the inputs need forensic checks and reconciliation with other data before they reach a model.

**Plan for no data-access right.** For business accounts at other banks, access depends on contracts with aggregators, which depend on contracts with banks, which now charge. Price and coverage can change without notice. A cash-flow model that fails silently when a large bank's data stops flowing is a model-risk problem.

**Keep reasons simple.** Even with disparate impact removed from Regulation B, the requirement to give specific denial reasons stands. Attributes that a borrower can recognise ("average balance below one month of payments") are easier to defend and to act on than opaque network signals.

**Expect reallocation, not just expansion.** The NBER paper shows that cash-flow models move approvals from some borrowers to others, including away from high-score young owners [5]. A credit team should measure who is newly declined as well as who is newly approved.

## Open questions and limitations

- **One research programme.** The FinRegLab and NBER results come from overlapping teams and, apparently, the same two fintech lenders' loans. There is no independent replication on bank or SBA data. The NBER paper is a working paper, not peer reviewed.
- **No public bank results.** JPMorganChase's use of Nova Credit is known only from a press report that does not say whether small-business credit is included [24]. This research found no US bank that has published how cash-flow variables perform in its small-business book.
- **Vendor claims unverified.** Every figure for Experian, Plaid and FICO products is the company's own, and the consumer products may never apply to business lending [18][20][21][22][23]. Experian's commercial attributes were announced in September 2026 with no named customers [18].
- **Section 1033 status after August 2026 is unknown.** The new proposal was at White House review as of the latest source found [14][15]; whether it has since been published, and whether it says anything about business accounts, could not be confirmed. The injunction date comes from a secondary tracker [13].
- **Interagency statement status.** Whether the 2019 interagency statement on alternative data remains in effect after the CFPB's 2025 withdrawals could not be confirmed. The OCC bulletin was read, but the statement's full text could not be retrieved [25][27].
- **Aggregator fees.** The terms of the JPMorgan data-access agreements are not public; the reports of agreements beyond Plaid rely on unnamed sources [16][17].
- **SBSS history.** The 2025 rise of the SBSS minimum to 165 comes from a lender-service provider's article, not an SBA source read for this paper [9]. Experian's description of 7(a) Small loan size conflicts with the SBA notice; this paper uses the SBA's $350,000 [8][33].
- **Loss outcomes.** No public source reports charge-off or yield outcomes for small-business portfolios underwritten with cash-flow models against a control.
